The Resistance of Social Art in the Capitalist Era

2025

When we think about art today, it’s not possible to separate artistic practices from the economic values they have. In the capitalistic era we are in, art has become a commodity, an object of the market; its value is based only on its costs and is determined by few auction houses, private collections and galleries. What I ask myself is: is it still possible to create art with social aims avoiding the market trap? When I refer to an art with social aims, I mean creating art aiming to have a positive outcome on people in more marginalized categories, on neglected places, on forgotten communities that, for one reason or another, cannot find the help they need in the Institutions or in the Government and that perhaps do not even care about art. With market trap instead, I refer to the fact that art is being phagocytosed by the market, becoming a mere object to sell, a concept to monetize, whose only value is the economic one. This process is called commodification of art and is strictly related to the market and the value of art. It’s the market today to establish an artwork’s value, its relevance, its worth. What I want to understand is if it’s really possible to create art aiming to solve social problems, to raise awareness on critical situations, without it becoming superficial propaganda with no concrete outcomes. In the following essay I will analyze the case of the Collaborative Project, whose impact was both positive and unsuccessful. I will focus on some artists that took part in this collective, such as: Christy Rupp, Justen Ladda and John Fekner. I will also examine cases when art opened the gates to the market and other phenomena that follow, like gentrification. This is the case of Banksy and Blu. I will finish with the collective WochenKlausur, who has always been committed to social causes.

Art not only follows and creates an aesthetic, but it has different intentions: entertainment, illustration, representation of certain political messages. It’s easy though that art becomes a means of trading and earning, that ends up feeding the market. There are some cases when artists manage to escape this mechanism and focus on the social sphere, on everyday problems affecting ‘normal’ people.

One of the cases is Collaborative Project, also known as Colab, a collective of artists that was founded in 1977 in New York. They were less interested in creating objects and more interested in questioning the role of the artists in the society, the context in which art is produced and exhibited and its nature. Their collective was artist-driven, without hierarchies, intended to create collaborative projects; it was indeed necessary for them to be completely autonomous from Institutions, NY museums or galleries, without relying on public grants. They gained their autonomy from the art world through a series of contributors affectionate to their work; and this was their strength. With that freedom they had the possibility to invent their own production and distribution system, not in opposition to the dominant art system, but through pretending it had never existed. In 1979 they organized one of the most groundbreaking underground art exhibitions that gave origin to a new style of art shows: The Real Estate Show . They set it up in the Lower East Side of Manhattan, in one of the many vacant buildings in their neighborhood, at 125 Delancey Street. The chosen location is emblematic, it perfectly represented the area: the neighborhood was traditionally home of lower-class immigrants and at that precise time was suffering a strong gentrification. To explain better: gentrification is a quick process of urban development during which a neighborhood changes from low to high value, causing displacement of a ‘poor’ community and long-term residents due to not-anymore affordable cost of living. Colab wanted to address this specific topic in their show and to raise awareness to the catastrophic phenomenon that was taking place. It’s exactly here that we see their commitment to the community. For them gentrification was just the tip of the iceberg, symbol of a larger discontentment with a society built on the marginalization of poorer and less represented communities. The Show was also dedicated to Elizabeth Magnum, a middle-aged African American resident in Brooklyn, killed by police as she resisted eviction. Another important aspect of Colab’s work is that it was conceived for an audience not necessarily acculturated about art, it was for everyone, even for passersby and people living in the neighborhood. The show opened on December 31st 1979, and featured works realized by anonymous amateurs, addressing how money controls where one can live and in which conditions, like an octopus that sneaks in between houses and buildings, as represented in the Show’s advertising flier made by Becky Howland.

After three days the location was shut down by the city’s Department of Housing Preservation and Development. The artists protested, also supported by the figure of Joseph Beuys. As a compromise, the city offered to let Colab choose another vacant building and they chose 156 Rivington Street, baptized ‘ABC No Rio’ that ran until 1985 as a centre for art and activism.

When I say that their intervention was also a failure, I refer to the fact that with the opening of the New Museum building on Bowery in 2007 and the opening of new galleries, the gentrification process in the Lower East Side has only strengthened. The Colab artists left Manhattan and moved to the South Bronx, where they found an even worse situation. Together with Fashion Moda, a storefront museum started by Stefan Eins, Colab co-organized ‘The Times Square Show’ in 1980. It hosted more than a hundred artists and proposed a new kind of exhibiting place. It was open 24 hours a day suggesting new forms of social interaction, and comprised a portrait gallery, a fashion lounge, and a souvenir shop selling cheap editions, readymades that proved how the difference between artworks and everyday objects is established by the art market.

Among the artists exhibiting there was Christy Rupp, with her series of ‘Rat Patrol’: posters representing life-size rats displayed inside the building. Her project began in 1979, when she started hanging posters (four thousand in total) around New York, mostly near garbage cans and rubbish sites. She developed this work during the three-weeks long garbage strike by NYC sanitation workers, during which the accumulation of garbage created new habitat opportunities for rats. At first Rupp started affixing the posters to mark areas that were infested, so people could avoid walking through dangerous areas, then she displayed them in places of commercial and political power such as around banks and on the steps of the City Hall to point out socio-economic problems and deterioration.

Remaining in the context of the exhibition ‘The Times Square Show’, let’s analyze the work of Justen Ladda, who created a huge illusory illustration in the basement, ‘Against Women reduction to a hole’. Entering from a side door the figure of a woman would stand and line up, surrounded by foot and handprints. His work is another example of public art operating in a dismissed and abandoned place, that underlines perfectly the neglected and deteriorated living conditions.

Another of his works is ‘The Thing’, created again in the South Bronx. It uses the same visual illusion as the woman and is drawn on a wall and seats of an auditorium in an abandoned school. The most interesting thing is that, to find the graffiti, you had to look for it. The path was challenging: you had to pass through decaying blocks, a flooded basement, a burned library, and finally you would get in the auditorium. The reason for that long route was to show and make evident the state of Western education and the economy in the neighborhood.

The strategy to highlight the poor condition of certain neighborhoods and the total neglect is the same used by John Fekner. His practice is ‘simple’, he describes the situation that he sees and that is occurring, by just writing ‘key-words’ on buildings. In 1980 in the South Bronx he realized ‘Broken Promises’, ‘Falsas Promesas’ , ‘Decay’, ‘Broken Treaties’ , ‘Last Hope’ and ‘Save Our School’ , all words written on the walls and buildings of Charlotte Street.

His intent was to draw attention to inadequate housing, poor services and the deplorable social problems afflicting the neighborhood residents for the past two decades, and it’s impossible to ignore his message.

To conclude, it is possible to say that the Colab artists’ approach, operating in dismissed and neglected places, was that of shining a light on critical situations, highlighting problems of those who did not have a voice to ask for help and justice, and those who were not being listened to.

Let’s see some examples where art was not able to escape the market and, willingly or unwillingly, became a product of it. First of all it’s the graffiti ‘Migrant Child’ created in 2019 by Banksy on the historic San Pantalon Palace in Venice.

The building has an artistic past: it has been the house for several artists that have occupied it and lived there, creating their works; but it is now abandoned, and it has been like that for years. The graffiti represents a child wearing a life jacket asking for help, holding in his hand a pink flare. A few years after its creation it is already in bad condition because of its positioning: being on the surface of the water that rises during frequent floods, it is often submerged and, as a result, faded. It is difficult to think that Banksy’s choice of location was random; it is much more likely that the ephemeral character of the work was intended by the artist. Nevertheless, debates about the restoration and conservation of the graffiti were immediately started and in March 2024 it was decided, with the will of Vittorio Sgarbi (Under-Secretary of State for Culture) and the support of the IFIS Bank, to buy the San Pantalon Palace. The goal is to make it an exhibition space to be added to the Gardens or the Arsenal during the Biennale, and to dedicate the space to young artists, with the possibility of preserving the graffiti. Leaving aside that the artist’s willingness to let the work deteriorate has not even been taken into account, the thing that aggravates even more the decision made is precisely the housing condition of Venice. In a city where housing is one of the main problems afflicting the population, where rents are unaffordable and people are obligated to occupy abandoned houses, was it really necessary to set up a new exhibition space, especially in a building that has been abandoned for years and is only taken into consideration now, when on its walls a work of high economic value has appeared? And this is where it is important to ask if as an artist, especially of the caliber of Banksy, it would not have been his responsibility to predict the consequences of such a work. Perhaps it was specifically made as a provocation, but is it right to always hide behind a provocation, thus escaping from the responsibility of the impact that a work has on society?

The second example is the case of Blu, the artist who refused to let his art become a commodity and refused to get involved in the process of gentrification. He is an Italian graffiti artist that in 2008 created ‘Brothers’ and ‘Chains’: two huge murals located on two buildings in Cuvrystraße, Berlin. After a few years, during the night between 11th – 12th December 2014 Blu destroyed his graffiti in Kreuzberg. His action was motivated by the changes that were carried on by the City Council in that area of Berlin: a real example of gentrification. He covered the graffiti in order to avoid it getting destroyed during construction and renovation, but overall in order to avoid it becoming a mere decorative piece in a soon-to-be-born cool district.

One of the arguments that could be moved against my initial criticism towards the art market is that art has always been a source of profit and has always been part of the economy. This is partly true: in the Middle Ages painters and sculptors created works of art only to make a living, with the difference that they were considered as craftsmen at the service of specific clients, like Kings and Popes. The concept of the art market as we understand it today has developed in modern times, with the end of patronage and the rise of private collecting and galleries, more exactly during the 19th and 20th centuries, when commodification of art increased, like for other aspects of life. With the rise of industrialization and capitalism, art became something to be owned, collected, a status symbol. This process has been accelerated by globalization because the international global market allows the cross-border buying and selling of art, turning it into a form of global capital. So, unlike the past where art could have political, ideological and religious functions, today it often ends up submerged by the logic of the global market, losing all other meanings. To conclude, I want to address the work by WochenKlausur, a collective of artists known for creating socially engaged, activist-oriented projects that intervene directly in the public sphere.

The group was founded in 1993 after 8 artists were invited to Vienna in 1992 to solve a localized social problem. This is precisely where the philosophy and art practices of WochenKlausur emerged: to implement minor but lasting interventions localized in favor of citizens. They articulate a radical rethinking of art’s role in society; for them there is no distinction between the artist who paints, and therefore whose goal is personal, and the artist who poses as mediator between social problem and solution. Indeed, they see the artist as a key figure in finding unconventional solutions that cannot be identified by orthodox methods. Their focus on real interventions moves away from aesthetic value and the art market, and instead works actively to improve the conditions of life for marginalized communities. As they said:

“Social renewal is a function of art. Art’s big chance lies in the ability to offer the community something that also achieves an effect. Art has political capital at its disposal that should not be underestimated. The use of this potential to manipulate social circumstances is a practice of art just as valid as the manipulation of traditional materials.” 1

Their first project was realized in 1993, Medical Care for Homeless People , after the invitation by the Vienna Secession. They wanted to provide medical care to homeless people that at the time gathered in Karlsplatz and that due to the exhausting bureaucratic process did not have access to the health insurance provided by the city. They decided to create a mobile clinic inside a Van with a physician treating all the patients, offering medical care free of charge, no insurance required. Everything was made possible thanks to donations and sponsors. After its realization the mobile clinic visits approximately 600 people per month. After 5 years the van was substituted by a larger one.

In conclusion, to answer my own question, there are some cases when it has been possible for artists to create an art useful for the social, that avoided the market and did not become a product of it, or that, although becoming part of the market, managed not to lose all its value. However, as far as it is possible, it means to compromise. It is clear that an artist who produces art without personal interest and therefore without personal income, makes sacrifices from a personal point of view. However noble the cause, it is not always possible because not every artist has the opportunity to create art without profiting from it; perhaps just because an artist only makes money from art; therefore it depends on each artist’s background. It is clear that there must be favourable conditions or the artist must be able and willing to put himself in unfavourable conditions. It is then easy to understand why many artists rely on the market. Whether or not it is right that an artist’s only way of earning is through selling and producing his or her works is another important question to ask.
