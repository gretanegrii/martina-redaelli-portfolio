Homelessness in milan: losing one's home and identity

This essay is the basis of the work Postcards They Won’t Receive

2025

“House is a building intended to provide shelter for human habitation, typically holding possession as well as persons, decorated and spatially designed in both interior and exterior and by which empty space is transformed into a residential one.”

In Milan, according to the results of the survey racCONTAMI 2024 (February 2024), there were 2,343 homeless people, a figure equal to 0.17% of the urban population.1 The condition of homeless people in Milan is a reality as visible as it is ignored, a silent presence that populates the streets, stations, parks, underpasses. They appear and disappear in the crowd, between the inattention and indifference of the city that runs fast. This ethnography is based on direct testimonies collected from documentaries, videos, interviews and field reports, giving a human, social and practical overview of homelessness in the Lombardy metropolis. The objective is to give a voice to those who, in the streets of Milan, live a daily life made up of struggle, adaptation, but also of relationships, strategies and hope.

What happens when you lose your home, your shelter and your daily life? As we will understand through the testimonies of Marco, Gianluca, Mario, Valentino, Max, Domenico, Alan and Angela (fictitious name), it also means losing one’s identity and access to social life and relationships. Becoming homeless means losing the right to live, but also the right to belong. It means becoming inhabitants of what Marc Augé2 has defined as “non-places”: transitional, anonymous spaces where no lasting links are established or forms of identity developed. The platforms, subway cars, underpasses and porches become temporary shelters, devoid of protection and meaning. The loss of a home also entails the loss of dignity, intimacy, daily routine and mental stability. A house is not just a roof: it is the symbolic space in which you build your self, organize your life, preserve your memory.

Urban context: Milan and homelessness

The city is equipped with a network of services that includes canteens, dormitories, solidarity clinics, listening centers, but these tools often prove insufficient or unsuited to the real needs of the homeless population. Resources are limited, rules rigid, complex bureaucratic access. Moreover, the Milanese society itself oscillates between feelings of compassion and attitudes of exclusion. Many see extreme poverty as the result of wrong personal choices, vices or individual failures, feeding a stigma that further isolates those living on the street.

1. Feeding

Food is one of the primary needs for those who live on the street, but also one of the areas in which the city of Milan seems to be able to respond with a certain effectiveness, thanks mainly to the tireless work of associations such as Opera San Francesco, ProTetto3 and volunteers. Direct testimonials tell of a solidarity network which, despite a thousand difficulties, manages to guarantee warm and regular meals.

2. Sleep

Sleeping on the street is one of the most traumatic and complex experiences for those living in homelessness. It’s not just about finding a place to lie down; it’s about surviving the night. The cold, noise, fear, uncertainty, the possibility of being robbed or attacked are always present. Sleeping on the street is an act that requires continuous vigilance, a sleep never complete, always broken.

Many choose semi-hidden or peripheral places, such as parks or underpasses.

Public dormitories are an alternative, but they are not always perceived as safe or welcoming. Often people prefer the street to avoid environments considered degraded, violent, crowded.

During the Covid then the situation has made nothing but worse because the homeless people were completely abandoned by the city administration. There were only associations to help them by providing food but being forced to sleep on the street during a pandemic and with the city in full lockdown constantly put them at risk of infection.

3. Hygiene and health

Personal hygiene for people living on the streets is a daily challenge. It is not simply a question of cleanliness: it is a question of dignity, physical and mental health, protection from stigma. Personal hygiene is one of the main requirements that in our society allows you to access social life and relationships, as lack of hygiene accelerates the disintegration of identity.

During the lockdown, with the closure of bars, stations and centres, access to toilets became almost impossible. Many had to urinate or wash outdoors, exposing themselves to health risks and further humiliation. This invisible everyday life, which for most people is taken for granted, for those who live on the street is a constantly denied need.

Access to care is complicated: those who do not have a domicile or documents often cannot access continuing care, medicines and specialist visits. Some associations offer free clinics, but they are not always able to cover the needs. In addition, many homeless people develop a mistrust of health institutions, fuelled by experiences of rejection or neglect.

4. Security and violence

To make the situation even more difficult is added the danger of the road, not only for the climate, but for the gratuitous violence, thefts and assaults.

What characterizes the experience of a homeless person is the search and reconstruction of a kind of home or family environment that resembles a dwelling even in the street. Creating spaces of micro intimacy, a cart full of objects is not a waste but becomes a personal archive; a mattress under a bridge is not a single sign of discomfort but a survival strategy; a cardboard hidden around a street corner is not a waste but the bed for the next nights. The subject of violence is closely related to the dormitories that become places with a high density of crime and violence becoming less safe than the street.

5. Mental health and resilience

Life on the street has devastating consequences for health, both physical and mental. The cold, sleep disruption, malnutrition and exposure to weather make homeless people prone to premature ageing and chronic diseases. But even more serious are the psychological effects: depression, anxiety, sleep disorders, addictions.

Loneliness and psychological suffering are two reasons for homelessness and are also two consequences of homelessness. Many have experienced childhood trauma or early incarceration, like Alan, who recounts the failure of host communities.

6. Identity, stigma and invisibility

The greatest difficulty that is repeatedly witnessed by homeless people is the relationship with other people living in a normal condition. The common feeling is to ignore, look away, pretend that these people do not exist.

In 1966 Mary Douglas4 deepened the study of notions of purity and contamination in human societies, introducing a key concept: dirt is something out of place. What we consider dirty, disturbing or unacceptable is not in itself but it is because it breaks the cultural order that we expect. In this sense, a homeless person, whom we can meet and recognize on the street, in an urban context like Milan is perceived as out of place, as wrong, even as annoying because it interrupts the spatial order. By sleeping in a place not intended to inhabit, he breaks the aesthetic and moral norms; moreover, he does not produce, consume or follow the rhythm of the city. Above all it makes visible the vulnerabilities of society that society wants to hide.

Often the real primary need that is missing is social interaction, consideration, to be seen as human, male or female, and not as waste. Be seen and not invisible.

Social stigma reinforces self-exclusion. Those who live in the streets often internalize the negative image that others project on them, and stop looking for help, affection, hope. Regaining an identity is one of the most difficult tasks on the reintegration path.

Conclusion

The stories collected show that homelessness is not a choice but a condition, often the result of personal or systemic crises. The street is often the only solution, but it cannot replace a home. Social stigma reinforces self-exclusion. Those who live in the streets often internalize the negative image that others project on them, and stop looking for help, affection, hope. Regaining an identity is one of the most difficult tasks on the reintegration path.

The first step to change is listening: only by knowing these invisible lives can we begin to include them.
